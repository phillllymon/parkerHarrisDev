RACEMATE

  • TypeScript
  • React
  • Express
  • AI-assisted development
livegithub

RACEMATE is a web app for managing sailboat races, from checking boats in to calculating final results.

Race committees often work with clipboards and stopwatches. Recording finishes can take three people: one to watch boats cross the line, one to call out times, and another to write everything down. RACEMATE lets an operator stage approaching boats in a short, reorderable list, then tap each boat as it crosses to record its finish time.

The app also guides the start sequence with a countdown and timed reminders, records boats that cross the starting line early, and calculates corrected times and results using the selected scoring method.

I’m developing RACEMATE with Seattle’s Center for Wooden Boats, where it is already being used to manage races. Feedback from the race committee is driving an ongoing effort to make the interface more intuitive and handle unexpected actions—such as recording a finish before a race has started—without producing inconsistent results.

RACEMATE has also been a hands-on education in AI-assisted development. I’ve used Claude Code extensively and learned that it works best when I first lay out the design, work through the data flow, and make specific implementation requests. I use AI to help build the app while keeping responsibility for product decisions and how the pieces fit together.

BeatBurner

  • JavaScript
  • Web Audio
  • Capacitor
  • PHP
livegithub

I built BeatBurner because I enjoy rhythm games and wanted to play with my own music. It uses JavaScript’s Audio Context to analyze audio and generate playable levels, synchronizing moving tiles with the music.

Automatically generating rhythm-game levels from multiple audio sources presented a host of engineering challenges. For example, the live radio feature streams music from online sources, but the incoming audio wasn’t directly compatible with JavaScript’s Audio Context analysis tools.

To solve this, I built a pipeline that divides the stream into ten-second chunks, plays and records each chunk internally without audible output, and analyzes the recording to generate tiles. The audio then plays to the user with a delay, synchronized with the moving tiles. This process runs continuously, making the separate clips sound like one uninterrupted stream.

Keeping all those pieces synchronized—including when a player pauses and resumes the game—was a significant challenge, but the kind of challenge I thrive on. It’s incredibly satisfying to see all the moving parts of a complex system come together.

BeatBurner supports built-in songs, user-uploaded music, live radio, and audio sent through a connected computer—including music from streaming services.

GRAFFITI

  • JavaScript
  • PHP
  • SQL
  • Browser extensions
livegithub

GRAFFITI is a browser extension that adds a shared comment section to any webpage. It was my first major independent project after leaving AWS, revisiting an idea I originally built more than a decade earlier.

The idea started with Craigslist. If I went to see an item for sale, I wanted to leave useful context for other buyers: “Looks great in person,” or “The photos don’t show its current condition.” I couldn’t add features to Craigslist itself, but I could store comments separately, associate them with page URLs, and retrieve them through an extension whenever someone visited that page.

GRAFFITI takes that idea beyond classifieds. I built the extension’s interface and a PHP API backed by a SQL database, focusing on making the commenting experience clear and easy to use. The architecture was straightforward; the satisfying work was bringing the design and interactions together into a finished product.

GRAFFITI is live and available to use. It works well, and I’m proud of the finished product's clean interface and smooth integration of features.

Wind & Waves

  • JavaScript
  • React
  • Canvas
livegithub

OK this one really brings out my inner nerd and channels my former life as a high school physics teacher. The forces that make a sailboat work are the subject of a surprising amount of debate, even among sailors.

I built this simulator to illustrate those forces - you steer a boat around the screen and you can see, in real time, arrows representing the forces on the hull, sail, and centerboard. And it's not just a diagram over a boat graphic; underlying the boat's motion is a physics model using those exact forces to calculate acceleration.

This one is an oldie but a goodie - I still love how I was able to write a simulation using JavaScript and basic physics and the result moves surprisingly like a real boat.

Who hasn't wished for a pirate-themed chess website?

Inspired by chess.com, CheckMatey is a fully-functional online community for chess players with multi-player functionality as well as single-player games against the computer. There's also a community forum where you can share your chess or pirate-related thoughts with other players. You can even use the move-recording feature to share that new opening you just invented.

If your opponent's not online when you want to play, just use the "invite a friend" feature to challenge your friend via email. If you're still online when your friend clicks the link, you'll automatically be put in a game together. Of course, if you prefer Tetris to chess, we've also got you covered.

CheckMatey has a Ruby on Rails backend and a React & Redux frontend.

SimpleChessMove

  • PHP
  • JavaScript
  • CSS
livegithub

Projects such as CheckMatey made me wish for a publicly facing chess API, but I couldn't find one. Why isn't there a simple endpoint where I can describe a game state and it suggests a move?

So I built one. It's a public facing API for chess - you can use SimpleChessMove to either suggest a move or to check if a move is legal. This way anyone building a chess application online doesn't have to implement the chess logic - why solve that problem more than once?

The user interface is simple: just a chess board where you can play against the computer. More interesting is the detailed documentation for use of the API and the section that allows you to adjust parameters and query the API to simulate what your own chess application will do. The algorithm is simple: it looks ahead the requested number of moves, maximizing the piece point advantage or, if possible, winning the game. Check it out and see if you can beat level 4 or 5.

Don't Die Together! is a top-down, multiplayer zombie survival game. You start off with a pistol and, with zombies closing in from all sides, you must survive as long as possible. You can invite your friends to play in the same game with you, but be careful: the more players there are, the more zombies arrive!

Don't Die Together! uses MongoDB on the back end and React on the front end. Multiplayer functionality is through Socket.IO. The game rendering uses HTML5 canvas.

Other collaborators on this project are Ryan Sabik, Wesley Tam, and Ryan Sullivan.

Box truck conversion

Building on skills I developed building my van conversion I converted this former uhaul truck over the course of two years starting in 2020. Although the overall length is almost identical to the cargo van, the box width and height meant I could fit far more amenities. It's insulated on the floor, walls, and ceiling with 2" R10 foam and features a propane heating system as well as a tiny wood stove - it's fantastic for winter trips.

I was working from home for AWS at the time, so I knew I needed a big desk with plenty of power so I included 600 amphours of battery which could be charged by plugging in, running the enginer, or from 200 watts of rooftop solar. In addition to the main, full sized bed there's a couch that easily converts to a single bed or a table, a galley with propane stove, microwave, and a big sink, and a bathroom with tiled floor and a shower. The pressurized hot and cold running water is supplied by 80 gallons of fresh water storage.

Building this truck is one of my favorite projects. Figuring out how to integrate systems such as plumbing, propane, and electrical and getting them to all work together in a space that feels warm and inviting was a wonderful challenge. I've driven this truck all over the country and lived in it quite comfortably (along with my cat!) for several months at a time.
